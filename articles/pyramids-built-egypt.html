The Pyramids Built Egypt

The tomb was the excuse. The state that had to exist in order to build it was the product. We are stacking stone again.

October 2026 · Misha

The Great Pyramid is about 2.3 million blocks stacked 146 meters high. It was the tallest thing humans had made for more than 3,700 years. It was built for one body. No body has ever been found in it.

As a tomb, it failed. As a project, it produced the first large territorial state run by professionals.

The Egyptologist Mark Lehner, who spent decades digging up the town where the builders lived, has put it the way this essay will: Egypt did not simply build the pyramids. The pyramids built Egypt.

First courseThe stone asks questions

Try to stack 2.3 million blocks in one reign and the stone starts making demands.

You need limestone from Tura, across the river. Granite from Aswan, 800 kilometers south. Copper from Sinai for the tools. Cedar from Lebanon; the royal annals record Sneferu importing forty shiploads. So you need ports, boats, quarry expeditions, and a foreign policy.

You need to feed the crew. Herodotus said 100,000 slaves. The archaeology says something closer to 20,000 to 30,000 workers, rotated, housed, and fed well. Lehner's site at Giza, a workers' town from a few reigns later, turned up industrial bakeries and enough animal bone to suggest more than 4,000 pounds of meat a day for a crew of about 10,000. That meat came from somewhere. So you need estates in the Delta, herds, a census to count them, and granaries to hold the surplus.

None of this existed at that scale before. The pyramid pulled it into existence. A country that had been a string of river villages under a god-king became a logistics network with a tax base.

Second courseSneferu's three drafts

Khufu gets the credit. His father did the R&D.

Sneferu built three giant pyramids. Together they contain more stone than the Great Pyramid. The first, at Meidum, was reworked and eventually shed its outer casing. The second is the Bent Pyramid: it starts at about 54 degrees and, partway up, flinches to 43. Somebody looked at the cracks and changed the plan while the building was going up. The third, the Red Pyramid, uses the safe angle from the ground.

That is one reign, three attempts, and a visible learning curve in limestone. By the time Khufu's crews start at Giza, they can level a 13-acre base to within a couple of centimeters and point it at true north with an error of a fraction of a degree.

The knowledge did not exist before the project. It was a byproduct of failing at full scale.

Third courseMerer's logbook

In 2013, at an ancient harbor on the Red Sea, Pierre Tallet's team found the oldest inscribed papyri ever recovered. They are not a prayer or a king list. They are a timesheet.

An inspector named Merer ran a crew of about forty. His logbook records, day by day, hauling limestone from Tura to Giza by boat in the last years of Khufu's reign. Load, sail, deliver, return. It notes the day a boat was sent to fetch the crew's food.

The oldest surviving papyrus document in the world is middle management reporting upward on a pyramid job.

That is the second thing the pyramid built: a habit of writing things down. Counting, measuring, scheduling, auditing. Work gangs were split into rotating units with names; their graffiti survives, including a crew that called itself the Drunkards of Menkaure. Somebody tracked which gang moved which block. Writing existed before the pyramids, but the pyramid age is when it became the operating system of the state.

Fourth courseThe engineer beside the throne

The first pyramid has a name attached that is not the king's. On the base of a statue of Djoser sits the name and titles of Imhotep, his chancellor. He is not known to have been royal. Two thousand years later Egyptians were worshipping him as a god.

Under Khufu the top jobs still went to family. His vizier and likely architect, Hemiunu, was a relative. The man running the Giza harbor in Merer's logbook, Ankhhaf, was the king's half-brother.

Then the workload outran the bloodline. By the Fifth Dynasty the high offices were going increasingly to men from outside the royal house, promoted because they could run things. By the Sixth, an official named Weni could start in a minor post and end as governor of Upper Egypt, and carve the whole career on his tomb wall.

The pyramid needed competence more than it needed cousins. So competence moved toward power.

Fifth courseGigawatts of limestone

Now the stone is silicon and the afterlife is called AGI.

After this summer's earnings calls, Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet and Meta were guiding to more than $735 billion of capital spending for 2026, against roughly $410 billion the year before. A trillion in 2027 is the working assumption on Wall Street. Whether the god arrives is, for this argument, beside the point. Look at what the project is demanding on the way.

The United States is relearning how to build power plants and chip fabs because the stone asked for them. Export-control officials now write rules in units of compute. Governments that had no technical staff are hiring them, because you cannot regulate, buy, or fight with something you cannot read. The engineer is back beside the throne.

And the knowledge aggregation is no longer a side effect. In Egypt, building the monument happened to concentrate what people knew. Here, concentrating what people know is the monument.

Sixth courseThe digital tomb

Strip away the theology and a pyramid is a memory machine. Everything in it exists to stop one person from being forgotten.

The body is preserved. The name is cut into stone, because for Egyptians an erased name was an erased person. Djoser's statue sits sealed in a stone box with two eyeholes drilled at the angle of the northern stars. Khufu's 43-meter cedar boat was taken apart into 1,224 pieces and buried beside him, ready for reassembly. By the end of the Fifth Dynasty the walls themselves start talking: the Pyramid Texts, hundreds of spells carved inside the burial chambers so the king's passage would not depend on any living priest remembering the words.

Read that as an engineering spec. Capture everything. Lose nothing. Make it independent of the people who currently hold it in their heads.

That is the spec we have handed to AI. We trained it on every book, forum and codebase we could reach. Then we noticed it forgot yesterday's conversation, and treated that as a defect to engineer away: longer context, persistent memory, whole inboxes and archives poured in. There are companies that will build a chatbot from a dead parent's messages.

The Egyptians wrote letters to their dead. They inked them on bowls and left them at the tomb, asking for help with an illness or an inheritance fight. Nobody wrote back.

We are building the tomb again. This time it is for everyone, and it answers.

CapstoneTranscend, or outgrow

There are two reasons to build something that remembers everything.

The first is the pharaoh's. Transcendence. One man stops being human and takes his place among the stars that never set. This is the AGI pitch in its honest form: a mind that is not bound by death, by forgetting, by a single lifetime. A successor.

The second is the scribe's. A human brain holds one lifetime, holds it badly, and takes it to the grave. Every generation starts close to zero. A master needs decades to make another master, and that has been the rate limit on civilization since the beginning. Writing was the first patch. Plato tells it as an Egyptian story: the god Theuth offers the king writing as a cure for forgetting, and the king replies that it will make people forget more. Both were right. Egypt lost some memory and gained a state.

Egypt also tells us which of the two actually gets delivered.

The pharaoh paid for the first and got the second. Khufu did not join the stars. His granite sarcophagus is still in the King's Chamber, and it is empty. What survived was Merer's logbook, the surveying, the arithmetic, the civil service. Transcendence was the funding pitch. Memory was the deliverable.

Expect the same again. The people signing the purchase orders want the god. What everyone else gets is an archive that can talk: the knowledge of every craft, no longer dying with the people who practiced it. That does not leave humanity behind. It removes its oldest limit.

After the Fourth Dynasty the pyramids got smaller and the masonry got worse. Egypt kept growing. The tomb had become optional. The thing it built had not.
